# Managing people

Read this when: someone is starting, expectations or feedback are due, work needs delegating,
performance is slipping, someone has to leave, payroll and employer registrations are being set
up, the team is passing ten people, or the question is whether to add HR help.

Engineer-specific onboarding and growth → `0x:engineering-leadership`. Employment law differs
sharply by country and US state. Any termination, and anything involving a complaint, leave,
health or a protected characteristic, needs an employment lawyer before action, not after.

## Onboarding to productive in two weeks

Preboarding, finished before day one:

- Signed contract, PIIA, tax and right-to-work forms. In the US, the employee completes their
  part of Form I-9 by the first day and the employer verifies documents within three business days.
- Accounts through SSO with least privilege, password-manager access, equipment delivered.
- An onboarding page: the strategy page, the handbook, who owns what, the draft 30/60/90 plan,
  and a first task already chosen.
- A buddy who is not the manager, for the questions people hesitate to ask the founder.

| When | Goal |
|---|---|
| Day 1 | Setup done by midday; 30 minutes with the founder on the strategy and why this role exists; first task started |
| Days 2-3 | First small contribution delivered: a fix, a support answer, a corrected doc |
| Week 1 | Shadow customer calls and support; read the last month of written updates and decisions; agree the 30/60/90 plan |
| Week 2 | Own one small outcome end to end; write a note: what I learned, what confuses me, what I would change |

- The founder blocks real time for the first two weeks: a short daily check-in, then the weekly 1:1.
- The new person's two-week note is the best audit of the onboarding and of the company; newcomers
  stop seeing broken processes within weeks. Fix the onboarding page after every hire.
- Measure days to first contribution and days to owning an outcome. If either drifts, the cause is
  usually missing access or no chosen first task.

## 30/60/90-day expectations

Agree in writing in week one, derived from the scorecard outcomes (`hiring.md`).

| Mark | Pattern | Example: support lead |
|---|---|---|
| 30 days | Learning, first contributions, relationships | Handles the queue alone; knows every ticket category |
| 60 days | Owns the area with support | Response-time target met; help-center plan agreed |
| 90 days | Independent ownership, first measured result | Top articles live; tickets per account falling |

- Review at each mark in a dedicated conversation: on track, at risk or off track, with evidence,
  then a written summary to the person.
- Where a probation period exists (common outside the US, often with shorter notice), hold the
  90-day review before it ends so the decision is deliberate. Some jurisdictions restrict extending
  probation. In the US at-will setting probation means little legally, and wording that implies
  "permanent" status after it can undermine at-will employment.

## One-on-ones

- Weekly, 30 minutes, with every direct report. Move it, do not cancel it: cancellations tell the
  person where they rank.
- The report owns the agenda in a shared running document; the manager adds items. Status belongs
  in written updates, not here.
- Questions that surface what matters: "What is in your way?", "What are you not getting from me?",
  "What are we doing that seems wrong?", "How is your workload?" Monthly: "Where do you want to be
  in a year, and what would help?"
- Record commitments and close them by the next meeting. A manager who forgets teaches people to
  stop raising problems.
- Weeks of "all good, nothing to discuss" often precede disengagement or resignation; ask directly.

## Delegation

Delegate an outcome to one named owner, not a list of tasks. The brief:

- the outcome and why it matters; how success is measured;
- constraints: budget, deadline, what must not happen;
- the decision level (below); the check-in schedule, set at the start.

| Level | The owner may | Use when |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Investigate and present options | New area, high stakes |
| 2 | Recommend; the founder decides | Learning period |
| 3 | Decide, then inform | Most work after the first 60 to 90 days |
| 4 | Decide without reporting | Routine and reversible |

- Say the level explicitly and raise it as trust builds. Delegate reversible decisions fully.
- When someone brings a problem, ask for their recommendation before giving one. Taking the problem
  back ("whose monkey is it?") turns the founder into the team's bottleneck.
- If the result is acceptable but not how the founder would have done it, let it stand and give
  feedback. Every silent redo or overrule inside the delegated level teaches the team to wait.

## Feedback

- Give it within days, specifically. Criticism in private; praise specific and often public.
- Situation, behavior, impact, then a question: "In Tuesday's customer call, you committed to a
  date before checking with engineering. We now have to walk it back with the customer. What
  happened?" Then agree the next step.
- Frequent small feedback beats rare large feedback. A problem must never first appear in a formal
  review or a performance plan.
- Ask for feedback on your own management at regular intervals and act visibly on some of it.

## Underperformance to a resolution

Diagnose before acting:

| Cause | Sign | Response |
|---|---|---|
| Unclear expectations | Working hard on the wrong things | Rewrite expectations; often the manager's miss |
| Skill gap | Effort, but quality is low | Pairing, training, narrower scope, a time limit |
| Capacity or personal | Sudden drop | Ask; adjust temporarily; leave and accommodation law may apply, so involve a lawyer |
| Motivation or fit | Disengaged, missed commitments | Direct conversation about whether the role is right |
| Wrong role | Strengths lie elsewhere | Move only if a real role exists |

Resolution path:

1. A direct conversation naming the gap with examples, followed by a written summary.
2. No improvement within two to four weeks: a written plan with two to four measurable criteria,
   the support offered, a 30 to 60-day window and weekly check-ins.
3. At the end: criteria met, the plan closes; not met, separation.

- Use a plan only when improvement is possible and wanted. If the decision is already made, a
  pretend plan wastes weeks and feels dishonest; negotiate an exit with severance and a release.
- Document the same way for everyone. Inconsistent treatment is the raw material of discrimination
  claims.
- Rule of thumb from experienced founders: by the time the question "should they go?" is asked,
  the answer is usually known. The common error is waiting too long, at the team's expense.
- Before acting, check for a recent complaint, protected leave, disability, pregnancy or
  whistleblowing. Any of these near a termination creates retaliation risk.

## Letting someone go

| Setting | What matters (mechanism; confirm current law) |
|---|---|
| US, at-will in most states | The reason must not be discriminatory or retaliatory; final-pay timing is set by state law, some requiring payment on the last day; benefits continuation notices; a release of claims needs consideration beyond what is already owed; releases of age claims from workers 40 and older need 21 days to consider and 7 to revoke (longer for group programs) |
| UK | A fair reason and fair procedure once the employee has the qualifying service, a rule recent legislation is changing; a settlement agreement binds only if the employee received independent legal advice |
| Germany | Dismissal protection after six months in businesses above a small-business threshold; written notice with a handwritten signature (email is invalid); consult the works council if one exists |
| Other EU countries | Statutory notice, sometimes statutory severance, consultation for collective dismissals |
| Contractors | Contract notice terms; a contractor who was in practice an employee may claim employee rights |

Sequence:

1. Prepare: lawyer review, separation letter, severance and release, final-pay calculation,
   benefits information, equity status (vested options and the exercise window), access revocation
   plan, handover plan, and the words for the team.
2. Meeting: early in the week, private, by video or in person, 10 to 15 minutes, with a second
   company person present where appropriate. The decision in the first sentence, a brief reason,
   no debate, then severance and next steps, then the letter. Let them react.
3. Access: revoke during the meeting, coordinated: SSO, admin consoles, code hosting, finance,
   customer data, shared password vaults. Not before (they learn by being locked out) and not hours
   later.
4. Same day: tell the team briefly and respectfully, without the reasons; agree the wording with the
   departing person where possible. Tell customers who worked with them, naming the new contact.
5. Follow through: final pay on time, equipment returned, an agreed reference policy (often dates
   and title only), and an exit conversation if they want one.

Layoffs of several people are a different process with notice and consultation duties above size
thresholds; involve a lawyer from the start.

## Payroll, benefits and employer registrations

Register as an employer before the first payday, run payroll through a provider (or a PEO in the
US, or an EOR abroad), and have the founder check the first run line by line.

| Country | Registrations and duties (mechanism; confirm current requirements) |
|---|---|
| US | Federal employer ID; state withholding and unemployment-insurance accounts in every state where an employee works; workers' compensation; state new-hire reporting; Forms I-9 and W-4; quarterly federal payroll returns, annual W-2s |
| UK | HMRC employer registration before the first payday; payroll reported on or before each payday (RTI); workplace-pension auto-enrolment; employer's liability insurance; right-to-work checks |
| Germany | Establishment number from the Federal Employment Agency; employee registration with health insurers for social insurance; statutory accident insurance; monthly wage-tax filings |
| Elsewhere | Local payroll provider or EOR; the obligations follow the same pattern |

- US benefits: health coverage is the main decision (a small-group plan through a PEO or broker, or
  an arrangement that reimburses individual coverage). Several states require employers to offer a
  retirement plan or enroll staff in a state program. In Europe statutory benefits dominate.
- Leave and sick pay: write the policy in the handbook at or above the local minimum.
- US: a salary does not make someone exempt from overtime; exemption depends on duties and pay.
- Failure modes: an employee moves state or country without telling anyone (new registrations owed
  there); a contractor converted to an employee without registrations; a missed final-pay deadline.
- Founders' own salaries → `0x:startup-finance`.

## Org structure past ten people

Somewhere around eight to ten direct reports, the founder's one-on-ones and context sharing stop
working. Until then, stay flat.

- Write an ownership map: every outcome, system, metric, customer segment, vendor and recurring
  process has exactly one directly responsible person. Gaps and doubles show up immediately.
- Span: five to eight reports per manager as a rule of thumb; fewer for a first-time manager.
- First managers: managing is a different job. Promote people who want it and already help others,
  with a trial period and a way back to individual work without stigma. Do not use management as
  the reward for the best individual performer.
- Keep titles modest. A "VP" at twelve people blocks hiring an experienced leader above them later
  and forces a painful conversation.
- Under about thirty people, functional teams with clear owners are simplest; move to
  cross-functional teams per product area or customer segment when coordination between functions
  becomes the bottleneck.
- Reorganize rarely and in writing: each change costs weeks of momentum.

## A people lead or outsourced HR

| Headcount (rule of thumb) | Setup |
|---|---|
| 1 to 15 | Payroll provider or PEO, HR software for records, handbook, employment lawyer on call; the founder owns people operations |
| 15 to 40 | Add a fractional HR consultant; a recruiter first if hiring more than about one person a month |
| 40 and up, or several countries | First in-house people-operations generalist |
| Managers, levels and review cycles | A head of people owning performance, compensation and leveling |

- A US PEO co-employs staff, gives access to group benefits and handles payroll filings for a
  per-employee fee; leaving one later takes planning. An EOR plays a similar role abroad.
- Bring a people lead in earlier when hiring fast, operating in several countries, facing an
  employee-relations dispute or a works council, or when HR administration takes several founder
  hours a week.
- The first people hire is usually a hands-on generalist or a recruiter, not a strategic executive.
- Never outsource the judgment: who joins, who leaves, what the values are. HR runs the process.

## Ask the user

- In which countries and US states do people work, and are they employees or contractors? It
  decides registrations and termination rules; default to treating each location as needing its
  own setup.
- Has this performance problem been raised clearly and in writing? If not, termination is premature;
  default to a direct conversation and a written summary first.
- Is there any complaint, leave, health issue or pregnancy involving the person? It changes the
  legal risk sharply; default to stopping and calling an employment lawyer.
- What severance can the company afford, and is a release wanted? Default: offer severance in
  exchange for a release, drafted by a lawyer.
- Who owns HR administration today, and how many hours does it take? Default: payroll provider or
  PEO plus a lawyer on call until about fifteen people.

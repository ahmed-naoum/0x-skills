# Strategy and moats

Read this when: writing or revising the company strategy, asking "what is our moat", choosing the
first market and the next one, sizing up competitors, a competitor or clone has copied the
product, deciding what the founder should stop doing, or setting the quarter's goals.

## The one-page strategy

A strategy is a small set of choices that makes most later decisions obvious, including what the
company will not do. Write it on one page, date it, and reread it at every quarterly review. If two
reasonable people could read it and disagree about whether to build the next large feature, it is
too vague.

| Section | What it must say | Common failure |
|---|---|---|
| Customer | One segment specific enough to list twenty named prospects, and the moment they feel the problem | "SMBs", "developers", "anyone who..." |
| Problem and current alternative | What they do today instead (spreadsheet, agency, incumbent, nothing) and what that costs them | Describing the product instead of the problem |
| Wedge | The first product for that segment, narrow enough to be clearly the best option for them | A platform on day one |
| Why we win now | The insight or edge: something true about the customer that others miss, access, speed, cost structure | "Better UX", "AI-powered" |
| Moat we are building | Which durable advantage (below), what compounds it, the metric that shows it growing | Claiming a moat that does not exist yet |
| Business model | Who pays, for what unit, roughly how much; detail in `0x:pricing-monetization` | Revenue deferred to "later" with no test |
| Not doing | Three to five tempting things explicitly out of scope this year | Empty section |
| Bets and kill criteria | The two or three assumptions the plan rests on, and the result that would change course | Nothing falsifiable |

Rumelt's "kernel" is the test of whether the page is a strategy: a diagnosis of the situation, a
guiding policy for dealing with it, and coherent actions that reinforce each other. A list of goals
("reach $1M ARR, launch mobile, expand to Europe") is not a strategy: it has no diagnosis and makes
no choice.

By stage:

- **Prototype**: customer, problem and wedge carry the page; the moat line is a hypothesis.
  Revisit monthly, because the page will change.
- **Launch**: revisit quarterly. The "not doing" list now needs teeth: paying customers will ask
  for adjacent work, and each yes dilutes the wedge.
- **Scale**: the page feeds planning, hiring and the board deck. Keep it one page; the detail
  lives in the plans that cite it.

## Durable advantage

Early companies rarely have a moat. They have speed, focus and an insight incumbents ignore.
Features, AI features included, are copied in weeks. A moat is an advantage that grows stronger as
the company grows and that a well-funded competitor could not buy quickly. Helmer's "7 Powers"
frames each one as a benefit (lower cost or a higher price) plus a barrier (why rivals cannot copy
it). The table merges his list with the two advantages founders claim most: data and distribution.

| Advantage | Real when | Test | False version |
|---|---|---|---|
| Network effects | Each new user makes the product more valuable to existing users of the same network | Would users leave for an identical product with fewer users? Is the network local or global? | "Community"; a marketplace where both sides use every competitor at once |
| Switching costs | Leaving costs the customer migration, retraining, rebuilt integrations, or risk | Count integrations and the people whose daily work runs through the product | Lock-in through a hostile export; customers notice, and law limits it: GDPR portability for personal data, and the EU Data Act requires cloud and SaaS providers to support switching, with switching charges banned from 12 January 2027 |
| Data | Data generated by using the product improves it for the next customer, and cannot be bought or scraped | Plot quality against data volume; an early plateau means a shallow moat | "We have lots of data" with no loop back into quality |
| Scale economies | Unit cost falls with volume | Is a ten-times larger rival cheaper per unit? | Rarely relevant before Scale |
| Brand and trust | Customers choose or pay more without comparing | Branded search, direct traffic, win rate when the rival is cheaper | A good logo |
| Distribution | A channel rivals cannot rent: owned audience, search footprint, partner or marketplace position, embedded integrations | Share of signups from owned channels; cost for a rival to reproduce it | Paid ads, which anyone can buy |
| Counter-positioning | The incumbent cannot copy without damaging its own business model | What would the incumbent lose by copying? | Merely being cheaper |
| Cornered resource | Exclusive access: license, approval, key people, unique supply | Is it exclusive and renewable? | A partnership either side can end |
| Process power | Operations so refined that rivals cannot replicate them even when they see them | Does quality or cost hold when volume doubles? | Rarely achieved by small teams |

Rules:

- Name one primary moat and the mechanism that compounds it, and put work on it every quarter. Two
  half-built moats are none.
- For B2B SaaS, the most reachable combination is becoming the system of record for one workflow
  (switching costs) plus integrations and an owned channel. For consumer products and
  marketplaces, network effects built with local density first: one city, one niche, one community.
- Data processed on behalf of customers usually cannot be used to improve the product for others
  unless the contract and privacy terms allow it. Check before calling it a moat
  (`0x:privacy-compliance`).
- Measure the moat: cohort retention, net revenue retention, share of organic acquisition,
  integrations per account. If the metric does not improve quarter over quarter, the moat is a story.

## The wedge and the sequence after it

The wedge is the narrowest product and segment where the company is clearly the best choice and can
reach customers cheaply. Choose it by four tests:

1. **Pain**: frequent and urgent, and someone already spends money or time on it.
2. **Reach**: one channel the founder can work personally: one community, one job title, one
   integration marketplace.
3. **Margin of superiority**: ten times better on one dimension that matters to the segment, not
   somewhat better on everything.
4. **Exit path**: the wedge connects to a larger market through a shared customer, shared data or
   shared technology. A wedge that leads nowhere is a niche, which is fine for a small profitable
   business and wrong for a venture-funded one.

Expansion runs in two directions; pursue one at a time.

| Path | Means | Prefer when |
|---|---|---|
| Same customer, more products | Adjacent products sold to existing accounts | Retention and trust are strong, customers ask for neighboring jobs, acquisition is expensive |
| Same product, more customers | Next segment, size band or geography | The product needs little change and the next segment will be convinced by the first |

Pick the next segment by reference value: the best one is where buyers are persuaded by the current
customers' results (Moore's "bowling alley" of adjacent niches). Do not expand until the first
segment's retention holds; expansion multiplies whatever is broken. Choosing the problem and the
first customer in the first place → `0x:product-discovery`.

## Competitors and where not to compete

Map competitors by the customer's job, not by features. The list includes the status quo
(spreadsheet, agency, internal tool, doing nothing), which is usually the strongest competitor at
Prototype and Launch.

Procedure, half a day per quarter, not a weekly habit:

1. List the alternatives customers actually considered; ask in sales calls and churn interviews.
2. For each: target segment, pricing and packaging, distribution channel, what customers praise and
   complain about (reviews, forums, support communities), shipping pace (changelog), and hiring
   (job posts reveal priorities a year ahead).
3. Mark where each is strong and profitable: there it will fight back.
4. Choose the ground: segments it underserves, deals it considers too small, and models it cannot
   copy without cutting its own revenue.

Do not compete:

- Head-on with a similar product in an incumbent's core profitable segment, at Prototype or Launch.
- On price, unless the cost structure is fundamentally lower. A price war paid for from runway ends
  when the runway does.
- Where buyers require certifications, integrations or support capacity the company cannot deliver
  this year.
- On feature-parity checklists, which let the rival set the roadmap.

Messaging and positioning against named competitors → `0x:growth-marketing`.

## When someone copies you

Classify what was copied before reacting. Functionality and ideas are generally free to copy unless
patented; expression (text, code, images) is protected by copyright and identity (name, logo) by
trademark. In parts of Europe, unfair-competition law (French parasitism, German rules on slavish
imitation) and unregistered EU design rights on an interface's look can also reach a close copy;
ask a local lawyer before relying on them.

| What was copied | Default response | Why |
|---|---|---|
| Features, workflow, pricing model | Ignore publicly; out-ship and deepen the moat | Not protectable; reacting advertises the copy |
| Positioning or page structure | Sharpen positioning on what the copy cannot claim: customers, results, integrations | Differentiation is the durable answer |
| Text, images, code, docs or design assets, verbatim | Enforce: copyright takedown to the host, then a lawyer's letter | Expression is protected by copyright automatically |
| Name, logo or a confusingly similar brand | Enforce trademark rights: platform complaint, registrar, letter, domain dispute | Confusion grows with time; delay weakens the position |
| Clone site collecting logins or payments | Treat as an incident today: abuse reports to host, registrar, CDN and browser safe-browsing services; warn customers | Customer harm is immediate |
| A large incumbent ships the same feature | Reposition to the segment it serves badly; lean on counter-positioning | Incumbent bundles are broad and slow to specialize |

Capture evidence before acting: dated screenshots, archived copies, WHOIS and DNS records, and the
dated original. Takedown and trademark mechanics → `incorporation-founders.md`; phishing response →
`0x:web-security`.

Out-shipping means shipping what a copy cannot reproduce quickly: integrations, features fed by
usage data, customer-specific workflows, service quality and speed of support. Racing on surface
features usually helps the copier, who skips the discovery cost.

## Do it yourself, automate, outsource or hire

The founder's hours are the scarcest input. Decide per recurring activity:

| The activity is... | Default | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| New and not yet understood | Do it yourself until a written playbook exists | First sales calls, first support tickets, first campaigns |
| Rule-based, frequent, low judgment | Automate: SaaS, scripts, agents with human review | Invoicing, receipt matching, onboarding emails, triage |
| Specialist, episodic, needs credentials | Outsource to a professional | Legal, tax filing, payroll, brand design |
| Recurring, core to the advantage, judgment-heavy, at least half a person's time | Hire | Core product engineering; sales once the playbook works |
| Not moving any goal | Stop | Vanity content, unused features, low-margin custom work |

- Do a function before delegating it. The founder has to know what good looks like to write the
  scorecard, judge a vendor, or notice a bad hire.
- Value a founder hour by what the founder's best use of it produces, not by salary. If a
  contractor removes a task for less, buy it.
- Automate a process only once it is stable; automating a changing process locks in the wrong steps.
- Order hires by the bottleneck to this quarter's objective → `hiring.md`. Back-office automation
  → `operating-cadence.md`.

## Quarterly goals

Use objectives and key results, few enough to remember without looking.

- Solo or two to three people: one objective, two or three key results. Up to about ten people:
  two or three company objectives. Each extra objective dilutes the others.
- **Objective**: qualitative and traceable to the strategy page ("Become the default tool for
  agencies under 20 people in the US").
- **Key result**: an outcome with a baseline, a target and a date, measurable without debate
  ("Paying agency accounts from 18 to 45", "Week-4 retention of new accounts from 40% to 55%").
  "Launch the integration" is an initiative serving a key result, not a key result.
- Label each objective **committed** (expected in full) or **aspirational** (partial is a good
  result). Unlabeled mixtures make grading meaningless.
- One owner per key result. Shared ownership is no ownership.
- Weekly: current value and a confidence level for each key result, in the written update. Quarter
  end: grade, write one paragraph on each miss, and carry anything forward only on purpose.

Traps:

- Output key results (posts written, features shipped) instead of a change in customer behavior or
  revenue.
- Planning at full capacity. Subtract support, maintenance and incidents first; in a small team
  they take a large share of the week.
- Goals that trace to neither the wedge nor the moat: the quarter then drifts toward whatever the
  loudest customer asked for.
- Rewriting key results mid-quarter to make them pass. Change them only when the strategy changed,
  and record why.

## Ask the user

- Who exactly is the customer, specific enough to name twenty of them? Everything else depends on
  it; default to the segment of the most engaged current users.
- What will you refuse to build or sell this year, even if a customer asks? Without it the strategy
  cannot guide; default to everything outside the wedge segment.
- Venture-scale company or profitable small business? It changes wedge size, moat urgency and
  expansion pace; default to profitable-first until stated otherwise.
- For a copycat: what exactly was copied, and is the brand registered? It decides ignore versus
  enforce; default to collecting evidence and saying nothing in public.
- How many hours a week do you want to work, and on what? It sets what to automate, outsource or
  hire first; default to protecting time with customers and on the core product.

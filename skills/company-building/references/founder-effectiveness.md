# Founder effectiveness

Read this when: the constraint is the founder: no time, too many roles, burning out, isolated, in
conflict with a co-founder, hiring for an unfamiliar function, separating from a co-founder,
outgrowing the coding role, or planning to step aside.

## Splitting the week

Rule-of-thumb split by stage, to compare against reality:

| Stage | Build | Sell and talk to customers | Operate (admin, hiring, finance) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Prototype | 60-70% | 20-30% | about 10% |
| Launch | 40-50% | 30-40% | 15-20% |
| Scale | under 20% | about 30% | 50% or more: hiring, managing, planning |

- The technical founder's trap is retreating into code when selling feels uncomfortable. Product
  progress without customer contact builds the wrong thing faster.
- Run a time audit before changing anything: two working weeks logged in 30-minute blocks, then
  compared with the intended split. Estimates of one's own time use are unreliable.
- Calendar architecture: maker blocks of three to four hours, usually mornings, with no meetings;
  customer calls, admin and meetings batched into fixed afternoons or theme days.
- Set a weekly floor for customer contact (a number of conversations or demos). It is the first
  thing to disappear in a busy week and the most expensive to lose.
- Each week, name the one result that makes the week a success, tied to the quarter's objective.
- Admin in one weekly batch; automate or outsource the rest (`operating-cadence.md`).

## Energy and burnout

The company is a multi-year effort; plan the founder's energy the way runway is planned.

The WHO describes burnout as the result of chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully
managed, with three dimensions: exhaustion, mental distance or cynicism about the job, and reduced
professional efficacy. Early signs, in oneself or a co-founder:

- dread before the week starts; worsening sleep; cynicism toward customers or the team;
- avoiding decisions or the inbox; irritability; longer hours with less done;
- exercise, friends and family dropped first.

Practices that hold over years:

- Sleep and exercise protected like production. Decision quality falls with sleep debt.
- One full day a week without work notifications. At least one real vacation a year of one to two
  weeks, with the deputy covering.
- Night alerts only for real outages; everything else waits for morning (`0x:site-reliability`).
- Sprints (launch, fundraise) get an end date and scheduled recovery after.
- Keep an identity outside the company: a bad month for the company is not a verdict on the person.

"I am doing everything myself and burning out":

1. Two-week time audit; list every recurring task.
2. Cut the customers, features, channels and commitments that do not serve the quarter's objective.
3. Automate and outsource rule-based and specialist work (`operating-cadence.md`,
   `strategy-moats.md`).
4. Fund help. A part-time contractor for support or operations is often the best first spend;
   a price increase can pay for it (`0x:pricing-monetization`).
5. Lower the bar for a defined period: slower release cadence, longer support response times,
   stated publicly.
6. Talk to someone: peer founders, a coach, a therapist.

If the user describes hopelessness, being unable to function, or thoughts of self-harm, leave the
productivity framing. Take it seriously, encourage contact with a doctor or mental-health
professional, and point to a crisis line (988 in the US; the local emergency number or crisis
service elsewhere).

## Support network

| Who | Gives | How to engage |
|---|---|---|
| Peer founders at the same stage | Honest comparison, tactics, morale | A group of four to eight meeting monthly under confidentiality; accelerator alumni, founder communities |
| Advisors | Domain expertise, introductions | Written agreement, small equity vesting over one to two years, a stated commitment such as a monthly call |
| Mentors | Pattern recognition about the founder's own path | Informal; specific questions |
| Coach or therapist | Self-awareness, conflict, stress | Paid and confidential |
| Investors | Network, fundraising help, some advice | Monthly update; they have their own interests and are not a confidant for everything |
| Lawyer, accountant | Professional judgment | Engage before the problem |

- Build relationships before needing them: a short monthly update to advisors and mentors makes a
  later ask easy.
- Ask specific questions ("We are choosing between A and B because...; which would you pick?"). A
  general "any advice?" gets general answers.
- One person's experience is a sample of one. Gather several views on big decisions, then decide.
- Advisor agreements assign IP and vest (`incorporation-founders.md`). Skip the decorative advisory
  board of names who do nothing.

## Co-founder disagreements

Co-founder breakups usually come from unspoken issues compounding over months, not from one fight.
The usual topics: strategy, roles and decision rights, effort and commitment, money and equity,
pace and values.

- A weekly co-founder one-on-one with a running issues list. Either can add; nothing waits more than
  a week.
- Decision rights in writing (`incorporation-founders.md`): inside a domain the owner decides; across
  domains the CEO decides; reserved matters need both.
- A quarterly "state of us": still aligned on the goal, the roles, the commitment? Would we start this
  company together again today? What one thing would each change?
- Disagree in private, commit in public. Never argue in front of the team or use employees as
  messengers; side conversations with staff about the co-founder are a warning sign.
- Escalation: written positions and a structured conversation; then a session facilitated by a
  coach or mediator experienced with founders; then the board for changes to decision rights; then
  separation.
- "I am doing more than my share" is handled by vesting and roles. If one founder's commitment
  drops, renegotiate role and equity formally instead of carrying the resentment.
- Danger signs: avoidance, decisions relitigated, one founder deciding alone, unilateral messages to
  investors.

## Learning an unfamiliar function well enough to hire for it

1. Do the work at small scale for weeks or months: founder-led sales, support, a campaign, a
   quarter of reading the books with the bookkeeper. Learn the weekly work and its metrics.
2. Ask practitioners which two or three sources they actually recommend, and read those.
3. Interview five to ten practitioners a stage or two ahead: "How would you evaluate a candidate?
   What separates great from good? What should they achieve in 90 days? What are the red flags?"
4. Write the scorecard from those answers (`hiring.md`).
5. Have an expert friend co-design and co-score the work sample and deep dive.
6. Consider a fractional or contract specialist first; their work shows what the role really is.

The trap is hiring whoever speaks the function's vocabulary most fluently. Verify results with
former managers, and know the function's three to five key metrics and what a good first 90 days
looks like before the first interview.

## Negotiating a co-founder exit

1. **Prepare privately with a lawyer.** Read the stock purchase or shareholders' agreement, vesting
   schedule, charter, voting agreement, board composition rights, IP assignment and any employment
   terms. Establish: vested versus unvested shares; the company's repurchase right on unvested
   shares and the window within which it must be exercised (miss it and the right can lapse);
   acceleration clauses; leaver provisions; who appoints the board seat the founder holds.
2. **The conversation**: private, honest, with a concrete proposal. Expect, and encourage, the
   other side to take legal advice and time.
3. **The separation agreement**:

| Term | Common approach |
|---|---|
| Offices and board | Resigns as officer and employee at signing; the board seat depends on who has the right to appoint it, and is negotiated |
| Equity | Unvested repurchased per the agreement; any acceleration traded for the release; a voting agreement or proxy for retained shares where control matters |
| Release | Mutual release of claims where possible |
| IP | Confirmation that all IP is assigned; materials and devices returned |
| Confidentiality, non-disparagement | Mutual |
| Transition | Defined handover help, possibly a short paid consulting period |
| Announcement | Agreed wording and timing, attached to the agreement |
| Tax | Each side takes advice on repurchase and acceleration |

   A departed founder holding a large stake with no role ("dead equity") weighs on future rounds. A
   buyback, if affordable, or a voting arrangement reduces the problem; vesting exists to prevent it.

4. **Access handover**, in a planned window at or before signing: transfer ownership of the domain
   registrar and DNS, cloud organization, code hosting, payment provider, app store accounts, email
   super-admin and social accounts; change bank signatories and payroll access; update officer and
   director filings; remove them from the password manager and rotate every secret and key they
   held; recover devices. Do this cooperatively. A unilateral lockout before agreement only for a
   real security threat, and on legal advice.
5. **Announcement order**: board and major investors first (they may need to approve); then the team,
   the same day, live; then key customers and partners; then public, if at all. One agreed story,
   no blame, and what changes for each audience.
6. **Afterwards**: update the cap table, corporate filings, insurance and signatories; reassign the
   domains they owned.

## Stepping back from coding

Signals: pull requests wait on the founder; the founder is on the critical path; the team has more
than about five engineers; customer, hiring or fundraising work keeps slipping; coding has become
the way to avoid other work.

The CEO's irreducible jobs: set direction and strategy; recruit and keep the leadership team; make
sure there is always enough cash; communicate with the team, customers and investors. Everything else
can be delegated eventually.

1. Write a role document: what the founder will own, what they will stop, by which date.
2. Leave the critical path first: no feature with a deadline depends on the founder. Keep
   prototypes, internal tools, small fixes and technical due diligence.
3. Name a technical owner (tech lead or CTO) with decision rights over architecture
   (`0x:engineering-leadership`).
4. Keep a weekly technical-context slot: architecture decisions and incident reviews, not every
   pull request.
5. Redo the time audit after a month to confirm the shift happened.

Coding agents make it tempting to keep building. That is fine for prototypes and exploration, not
for the team's critical path or as a way to avoid the CEO work. A founder who would rather stay in
product or engineering than be CEO has a legitimate choice; that is a succession question.

## Succession or stepping aside

| Case | Plan |
|---|---|
| Unplanned absence | Deputy and continuity runbook (`operating-cadence.md`); estate plan covering the shares |
| Planned role change: founder becomes CTO, chief product officer or executive chair | Search for a CEO or general manager, run by the board where one exists; new role and decision rights in writing; an overlap period |
| Leaving the company | Sale (`0x:startup-finance`), a general manager with profit share, maintenance mode, or wind-down |

- Signals: the founder dreads the CEO job; the company needs skills the founder does not want to
  build; burnout persists after the load was cut; the board or co-founders lose confidence.
- With investors, CEO succession is a board decision. Raise it early as a plan, not as an ultimatum.
- With a new CEO in place, the classic failure is the founder still deciding informally. Write down
  which decisions, if any, the founder keeps, tell the team, and route everything else through the CEO.
- Stepping out of the role is not giving up ownership; keep voting control and board composition in
  view when negotiating the new arrangement.
- A solo bootstrapped founder: hire a general manager with profit share, sell the business (there are
  marketplaces for small software businesses), or wind down well: notice to customers, data export,
  refunds of prepaid terms, deletion (`0x:privacy-compliance`), and closing filings.
- A planned CEO transition usually takes months from decision to handover; start before exhaustion
  forces it.

## Ask the user

- How many hours a week are sustainable, and what do you want to spend them on? Default: protect
  customer contact, focused building time and one full day off.
- What did the last two weeks actually look like? Default: run a time audit before changing anything.
- What do you and your co-founder disagree about that has not been said aloud? Default: put it on
  the issues list and discuss it this week.
- For a co-founder exit: what do the vesting and repurchase documents say, and when does the
  repurchase window close? Default: have a lawyer read them before the first conversation.
- Do you want to stay CEO, or would you rather own product or engineering? Default: stay CEO and
  step off the critical path first.
